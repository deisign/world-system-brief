# OIL RETURNS. THE BOTTLENECK DOES NOT.

An oil market can return to an **aggregate surplus** while consumers still face tight diesel supply, depleted onshore inventories and unsafe maritime routes. The International Energy Agency's **13 November** report is a particularly useful test of this distinction: October oil supplies rose, oil held on water increased, but stocks accessible on land kept falling. An independent Reuters investigation into Chinese AI infrastructure illustrates a parallel problem: investment plans built around restricted processors are not verified chip deliveries. **These are two separate mechanisms, not one causal chain.** [WSB-0045-SRC-01; WSB-0045-SRC-03]

## 1. Oil balance: October's supply increase is not a new delivery today

The IEA's November Oil Market Report states that world oil supply increased by **2.3 million barrels per day** in **October**, reaching **105.4 million bpd**. Gulf exports recovered despite continued attacks on shipping. The report expects the market to return to a supply surplus in **the fourth quarter of 2026**. Its demand projection for the whole of 2026 is **102.3 million bpd**, 2.7 million bpd below 2025; next year's demand is forecast to rebound by 2 million bpd. The supply number is an October estimate, the balance is a quarterly projection and the demand number is an annual forecast. None is an observed change occurring on **13 November**. [WSB-0045-SRC-01; WSB-0045-SRC-02]

The key risk to the IEA's scenario is maritime continuity. Cargoes can be exported despite threats, but interrupted voyages, insurance and rates still shape what arrives and at what cost. A global crude surplus does not establish fully restored, safe and inexpensive passage through Hormuz.

[International Energy Agency, *Oil Market Report — November 2026*](https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-november-2026) · [Alex Lawler, Reuters, 13 Nov](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/world-oil-market-is-returning-surplus-gulf-exports-recover-iea-says-2026-11-13/).

## 2. Inventory accounting: 52 million barrels more, but where?

The IEA estimates **observed world oil inventories grew by 52 million barrels in October**, the first monthly increase since June. But **oil on water rose by 86 million barrels**. **On-land oil inventories continued to decline**, and the report says onshore holdings were at their lowest level since at least 2016. OECD stocks fell by 47 million barrels. Aggregate stocks remain **462 million barrels lower** than at the beginning of the Iran war. [WSB-0045-SRC-01]

The numbers are not contradictory. Floating stocks and stocks held in terminals move in different directions. Simply subtracting floating oil from an aggregate headline would also be an oversimplification unless all stock classifications and coverage match. The documented conclusion is narrower: **the positive global total is not evidence of a rebuilding of immediately available inland stocks**.

**System reading:** location, custody, transport time and product type matter more to usable resilience than a single worldwide inventory total.

[IEA, *November Oil Market Report*, inventories and oil on water](https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-november-2026).

## 3. Diesel remains a separate bottleneck from crude

The IEA identifies refined-product scarcity, **particularly diesel**, as the overriding problem. Its report puts European diesel close to **$180 per barrel**, approximately **77% above pre-war levels** at the time of writing, even after the price fell 11% from its earlier October peak. Global refinery runs fell to **81.3 million bpd in October** before a forecast rebound. Refining utilisation, plant configuration, regional shipping constraints and product inventories cannot be replaced by crude availability alone. [WSB-0045-SRC-01]

**System reading:** an additional barrel of crude does not instantly become a barrel of diesel at the destination market. Recovery needs refinery conversion, appropriate product yield, shipping and local delivery. The IEA expects forthcoming emergency diesel releases to help bridge the product gap; planned reserve releases are not the same as completed delivery on 13 November.

[IEA, *November Oil Market Report*, refining and products](https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-november-2026).

## 4. Chinese AI: the construction budget is not installed compute

On **13 November**, Reuters published a review of **more than 150** Chinese procurement notices, investment plans, contracts and disclosures. These point to AI projects with a combined reported value exceeding **23 billion yuan ($3.2 billion)** that are built around restricted Nvidia H100, H200 or H800 processors. This is a value of **projects and equipment agreements across multiple stages**, including construction, power infrastructure and other costs — **not a total for processors purchased**. [WSB-0045-SRC-03]

Reuters distinguishes planned facilities, contracted developments and working sites. Some projects may be redesigned around domestic accelerators; for other sites, the agency cannot verify whether the Nvidia chips ever arrived. Nvidia's claim about its current direct China market access and third-party data about shipments use different measurement bases. Nor does Reuters establish that the named projects were linked to allegations surrounding Nvidia's leadership or unlawful chip diversion. [WSB-0045-SRC-03]

**System reading:** access to advanced computing depends on contracts, export controls, equipment arrival, power and operational readiness. A financial commitment and a procurement specification cannot both be entered into the Ledger as physically delivered computing capacity.

[Eduardo Baptista, Reuters, 13 Nov — Chinese AI procurement](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/huang-exit-us-scrutiny-imperil-3-billion-chinese-ai-projects-reliant-nvidia-2026-11-13/).

## SYSTEM LEDGER · Seven dimensions

**Flows:** October crude production and Gulf shipments; floating and onshore oil inventory; refinery conversion into diesel; proposed, contracted and operating Chinese AI infrastructure dependent on processors and electricity.

**Dependencies:** Global stocks depend on the location and commercial usability of barrels. End-market diesel additionally depends on refinery output and distribution. Chinese computing projects depend on actual delivery of permitted accelerators and completed supporting infrastructure.

**Bottlenecks:** Persistent maritime-security and freight uncertainty; depleted on-land inventories; refining and diesel-product tightness; uncertain receipt of restricted AI chips by selected projects.

**Substitutability:** Refinery capacity, emergency diesel inventories and different crude grades are not one-for-one substitutes. Domestic AI processors may be alternatives for some projects, but no common cost, performance or replacement horizon is established in these reports.

**Leverage:** Port access, shipping risk, refinery availability and export licensing can influence delivered supply. The sources do not support a quantified new leverage shift on 13 November.

**Rent:** High product and freight prices, diesel refining margins, procurement delays and AI-hardware redesign costs may redistribute commercial margins. New rent capture by a specific actor is not measured here.

**Adaptation:** Gulf exporters rerouted cargoes and increased oil-on-water stocks in October; refineries and IEA members pursue product balancing and emergency releases; Chinese developers may redesign facilities. These are distinct activities at different evidence stages.
