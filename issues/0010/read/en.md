# Redundancy before normality

More oil is moving out of the Gulf. That does not mean the old system is back.

Saudi Arabia says the East-West Pipeline is now carrying 5.8 million barrels a day toward the Red Sea. At the same time, Middle Eastern crude and product exports continue to recover. The useful fact is not another day of oil-price movement. It is that bypass capacity is doing real work.

This strengthens the trajectory already visible in the ledger: the system is learning to route around a dangerous chokepoint. Hormuz still matters, but it matters differently when producers can push more barrels west and when emergency inventories sit behind the market.

The second buffer is policy. The IEA is discussing how to implement the G7 agreement to release 100 million barrels of diesel and crude stocks. Allocation among products and countries is still unresolved. That makes today's meeting implementation of the 2 October delta, not a new delta.

A new risk is forming in the Gulf of Mexico. Forecasts put a developing hurricane on a path toward offshore areas responsible for roughly 15% of US crude production and 5% of US gas production. Until production or refining is actually lost, WSB keeps this in DEVELOPING. Weather forecasts are not barrels.

There is another useful structural signal in LNG. Atlantic LNG freight has collapsed from the war-driven March peak even as European demand remains strong. Fifty-five new LNG vessels arrived in the first seven months of the year, while shorter Atlantic voyages increased vessel availability. Shipping scarcity has eased because transport capacity expanded faster than demand. Important, but the evidence reviewed today does not establish a 7 October state transition.
